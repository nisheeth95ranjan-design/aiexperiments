A tiger is not a water treatment plant, a carbon sink, or a flood barrier.

But the forest it inhabits is all three.

Understanding this is the precondition for understanding why coexistence is worth the difficulty it involves.

The disconnect arises when this value does not flow in the direction it was intended.

This is not the money that flows to the reserves themselves, it flows downstream, mostly to cities and government officials.

The communities most dependent on these forests are often the same communities most exposed to the tigers living in them, and least likely to receive the downstream economic benefits.

In documented cases, families waited 6-18 months for payments that covered less than 40% of their loss.

Leaving no incentive to try and save the beast.

WHAT CAN BE DONE?

The carrying capacity question is no longer theoretical. Tigers are in the fields. Humans are standing against one of the fiercest beasts.

The model was primarily built for recovery and not for saturation.

1.
Protected Corridor Management

To allow seasonal animal dispersion from one reserve to another through and not into human spaces.

Panna & Bandhavgarh, MP
Kaziranga, Assam
2.
Relocation Done With Consent And Fair Compensation

Relocation of villages from core zones, done with genuine consent and fair compensation, has worked in places like Kanha and Tadoba. But coerced relocation has backfired badly and created lasting resentment toward conservation.

Kanha, MP
Tadoba, MH
3.
Rapid Response Systems

Several states now run SMS and WhatsApp alert networks that notify farmers when camera traps or forest guards detect tiger movement near settlements.

It reduces the chance of accidental encounters during early morning agricultural work, which is when most fatal attacks happen.

Chandrapur, MH
4.
Coexistence Infrastructure

Solar-powered electric fencing around agricultural fields has dramatically reduced both livestock predation and retaliatory killing

Nagarhole and Bandipur, KA
Gir, GJ
5.
Changing The Political Economy Of Retaliation

Poisoning remains the primary method of retaliatory killing, almost always following livestock loss rather than human death. Poison is cheap, untraceable and effective. Forest departments rarely prosecute, and when they do the fines are trivial. Meaningful enforcement plus fast livestock compensation are the two levers that reduce poisoning — neither works without the other.

Nagarhole and Bandipur, KA
Gir, GJ

The Conservation Conundrum

India set out to save the tiger, and succeeded. But success has a cost that falls almost entirely on the people who live closest to these animals: farmers who lose livestock, villagers who lose family members, communities that never asked to live on the front line of a conservation triumph.

The question India now faces is not whether it can grow its tiger population further, but whether it can share the burden of that achievement more honestly and more fairly. A country that counts its tigers every four years must also start counting what it asks of the people who make that count possible.
